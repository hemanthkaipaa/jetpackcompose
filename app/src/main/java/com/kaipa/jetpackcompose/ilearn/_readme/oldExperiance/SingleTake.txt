Single Take Flow:

-Camera app captures 15-second video → raw frames
-Convert raw frames to YUV (ImageReader gives you YUV 4:2:0)
-Send YUV frames to MediaCodec encoder → H.264 bitstream
-Send H.264 bitstream to AI library (C++ .so)
-AI library processes and returns filtered outputs (best images, slow-motion frames, etc.)
-For highlight video: AI selects the best 5–6 seconds of filtered frames
-You have offline music audio in a folder (not captured, pre-recorded)
-Use MediaMuxer to stitch: highlight video (H.264) + offline audio track → final .mp4

How to explain this in the interview:

"Single Take is a camera feature where we capture 15 seconds of video.
We pull raw frames from the camera HAL, convert them to YUV format using ImageReader,
and encode them to H.264 using MediaCodec. Then we send the encoded bitstream to an AI library — written in C++
— which analyzes the frames and generates outputs: collage, slow-motion variants, and a highlight video.

For the highlight video, the AI filters down to the best 5–6 seconds.
We then attach pre-recorded music from our app's assets using MediaMuxer,
which multiplexes the video track and audio track into a single MP4 file at synchronized timestamps.
The challenge was managing buffer allocation — MediaCodec has limited input/output buffers —
and ensuring the state machine didn't drop frames while processing multiple outputs in parallel."

Raw frames vs YUV

"Raw" is ambiguous and interviewers will probe it. Two meanings:

Bayer RAW — sensor output. Single-channel mosaic (RGGB pattern), one colour sample per pixel, 10–14 bit. Needs demosaicing, white balance, tone mapping — that's the ISP's job.
Processed frames — what the camera HAL hands out after the ISP. Already YUV.

In your case the camera team almost certainly gave you post-ISP frames, so "raw" meant "unencoded/uncompressed," not Bayer.
Safer phrasing: "we received uncompressed frames from the camera pipeline and worked in YUV_420_888."

Why YUV, not RGB

Separates luma (Y) from chroma (U, V). Human vision is far more sensitive to brightness than colour, so chroma can be subsampled.
4:2:0 = full-res Y, quarter-res U and V. 12 bits/pixel vs 24 for RGB888 — half the memory.
H.264/HEVC encoders consume YUV natively. Feeding RGB means a colour-space conversion first, wasted cycles.
Camera HAL and MediaCodec both speak YUV. No conversion in the pipeline at all.

One-liner: "YUV 4:2:0 halves memory versus RGB and is what the encoder wants natively, so there's no conversion cost in the pipeline."

24 fps

Fine, but be ready for "why not 30?" Good answers: cinematic look for highlight/slow-mo outputs;
20% fewer frames to buffer and push through the AI library; the memory budget.
If the AI processing was the bottleneck, say so — that's the real engineering reason.

The memory constraint — this is your strongest material

Do the arithmetic out loud, it lands well:

1080p YUV420 = 1920 × 1080 × 1.5 = ~3.1 MB/frame
At 24 fps = ~75 MB/sec
15 seconds uncompressed = ~1.1 GB — over your entire budget

So you cannot hold the capture in memory. That's why the architecture is what it is: encode to H.264 on the fly, write to file,
then re-extract frames in bounded batches for the AI library. The pipeline design falls directly out of the memory ceiling.

Say it like this: "Our service was capped at 1 GB. A 15-second 1080p YUV buffer is over a gigabyte on its own,
so holding frames in memory was never an option.
We encoded to disk as frames arrived, then streamed them back through MediaExtractor in windows the AI library could handle.
 If we exceeded the budget the system would reclaim memory and the camera preview would stutter — user-visible immediately."

We never wrote a 1 GB file — that number was the uncompressed in-memory size,
which is exactly why we didn't hold it. What lands on disk is the H.264-encoded video.

The arithmetic:

Raw YUV420, 1080p @ 24 fps, 15 s ≈ 1.1 GB
Encoded H.264 at ~10–20 Mbps ≈ 20–40 MB for the same 15 seconds

So encoding is roughly a 30–50× reduction. That's the whole point of encoding on the fly rather than buffering.

What you keep afterward:

Original video — kept, it's a user-visible output. They expect the full 15 seconds back.
Highlight / slow-mo / collage — kept, those are the deliverables.
Extracted frames fed to the AI library — transient. Decoded in bounded batches, passed to the library, released. Never all resident at once.
Intermediate scratch — cache dir, deleted once the state machine finishes or the capture is cancelled.
